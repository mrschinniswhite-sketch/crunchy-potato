Most failed deep work blocks don't fail because of a notification. They fail because the first twenty minutes ask for something the rest of the day never asked for: sustained attention with nothing to react to. That gap is uncomfortable, and discomfort is where people reach for a phone.

## The block isn't a container, it's a transition

Treating a two-hour block as one long stretch of focus is the wrong model. The real unit is the transition into it — the five or ten minutes where your attention is still scattered across whatever you were doing before. If that transition isn't handled deliberately, the block spends its first quarter fighting itself.

A better model: treat the first few minutes as a separate, lower-stakes task. Not "start the report," but "open the file and reread yesterday's last paragraph." Small, mechanical, low-friction. It gives your attention something to do with its hands while it catches up to the room.

## The plan needs to survive contact with a bad sentence

Blocks also collapse because the plan going in was too vague to survive the first hard moment. "Work on the proposal" isn't a plan, it's a hope. The first time a sentence doesn't come out right, there's nothing to fall back on except deciding whether to keep going — and deciding is exactly the kind of effortful task that deep work is supposed to protect you from.

Write the plan as a sequence of checkable moves before you start: which section, in what order, and what "done" looks like for the first one. When a sentence goes wrong, you're not deciding whether to persevere — you're just doing the next item on a list you already made when you had more attention to spare.

## Environment does less than people think, and more than people admit

Website blockers and closed doors get credit for problems they don't solve, and no credit for problems they do. They won't fix a plan that's too vague or a transition that's too abrupt. But they do remove the cheapest possible escape route in the exact minute your attention is most likely to look for one — which is precisely the first twenty minutes.

The honest way to think about environment: it doesn't create focus, it raises the price of quitting for the next ten minutes. That's a real and useful thing. It's just not the whole job.

## Try this

Before your next deep work block, write down two things on a sticky note or the top of your document: (1) the exact, small, mechanical first move — not the goal, the literal first action — and (2) the next three checkable steps after it. Set a timer for twenty minutes. When you hit a hard sentence or an urge to check something, look at the note instead of deciding. Do the next item on it. That's the whole intervention — no app, no blocker, just a plan that already knew this moment was coming.

Most people find the block doesn't get easier. It just stops being a decision every few minutes, which turns out to be most of what was making it hard.
